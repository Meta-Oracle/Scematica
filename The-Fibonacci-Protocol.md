The Fibonacci Protocol

How Scematica taught a machine to recognize a good pool in the first three seconds of its life.


Why this document exists

Every trading bot faces the same impossible question at the moment a new token appears: buy or skip, decided in less time than it takes to read this sentence. Most bots answer it with a pile of ad-hoc thresholds, a minimum liquidity here, a maximum age there, tuned by trial and error until the operator gets tired. The Fibonacci Protocol is Scematica's attempt to answer that question with structure instead of guesswork. It is a scoring system that converts a handful of raw facts about a brand-new liquidity pool into a single number between zero and one, a number that expresses how much the pool looks like the pools that have historically paid, and it hands that number to the rest of the machine, including the reinforcement-learning agent, as a compact, meaningful signal. This article explains what the protocol is, why it is built the way it is, and how each piece contributes to the bot's ability to tell a runner from a dead pool before the crowd has even noticed either.


The problem: a window measured in seconds, buried in noise

When a project pairs its token with wrapped SOL on Raydium's automated market maker, the blockchain emits an initialization instruction, and the instant that instruction confirms, a short window of opportunity opens. Price discovery has not happened yet. Arbitrageurs have not re-priced the token. The earliest buyers capture the largest share of whatever pump follows. That window is measured in seconds, and it closes fast.

The data behind Scematica makes the urgency concrete. Across hundreds of consecutive trades, pools bought within five seconds of creation won around 38 percent of the time with a large average gain; pools bought between six and fifteen seconds old won only about 22 percent; between sixteen and sixty seconds, roughly 11 percent and slightly negative on average; and beyond a minute, almost nothing won. The win rate decays roughly exponentially with age. Freshness is not a preference. It is the single most important thing about a pool.

But freshness alone is a trap, and it is exactly the trap Scematica has fallen into repeatedly. If you reward only freshness and buy every new pool, you drown in dead pools, tokens that were created and then never moved, each one costing a small slice of capital as it exits at the automated-market-maker spread. So the real requirement is subtler than "be fast." The requirement is a scoring system that rewards freshness while remaining calibrated enough to reject the pools that are fresh but going nowhere. The protocol has to be both eager and discerning, in the same three seconds, on nothing but the information available at the moment of pool creation.


The insight: why the golden ratio keeps showing up

The name of the protocol comes from a pattern that kept appearing in the winning trades. The most profitable new-pool events clustered around the golden ratio, roughly 1.618, and around the Fibonacci numbers that generate it, in three different dimensions at once: in the size of the pool, in the speed at which liquidity flowed into it, and in how long the winning positions were held before their momentum peaked.

It would be easy to dismiss this as numerology, and it is worth being careful about the claim. The honest version is not mystical. The golden ratio is the limiting ratio of Fibonacci growth, the number that the ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers approaches as the sequence continues. And the formation of a liquidity pool is a growth process. It is a crowd aggregating around a focal asset, each new participant reacting to the presence of the ones before them, which is precisely the kind of self-referential accumulation that produces Fibonacci-shaped dynamics in everything from sunflower seeds to population curves. When buyers pile into a fresh pool because other buyers are piling in, the rate at which liquidity compounds tends to organize itself around the golden ratio. A pool that grows at roughly the golden ratio in SOL per second is compounding at the resonance frequency of an organic pump. Below that rate, early sellers overwhelm the trickle of new buyers and the pool stalls. Above it, the crowd has genuine momentum.

So the choice to use Fibonacci numbers as the protocol's landmarks is not decoration. It is a bet that the structure of crowd-driven liquidity formation has a natural scale, that the scale is the golden ratio, and that measuring a new pool against Fibonacci landmarks is therefore measuring it against the shape of a real pump rather than against arbitrary round numbers. Whether or not the underlying theory is perfectly correct, the thresholds it produces are calibrated against live results, so the protocol earns its keep empirically even where the story is a story.


The four signals

At the moment a pool is detected, the protocol extracts four quantitative signals. Critically, it needs nothing beyond the initial pool event itself, no waiting, no follow-up on-chain queries that would burn the very seconds the whole system is racing to preserve. Each signal is scored on its own scale from zero to one, and the four are then combined.

The first signal is pool size, and it carries the most weight, thirty-five percent of the composite. Let S be the pool's SOL-side reserve at detection. The protocol treats the band from eight to twenty-one SOL as the sweet spot and gives it a perfect size score, because eight and twenty-one are the sixth and eighth Fibonacci numbers and this band showed a win rate several times higher than pools outside it. A pool just outside that band, from five to thirty-four SOL, the fifth and ninth Fibonacci numbers, still scores well but not perfectly. The extended range from three to fifty-five SOL scores modestly. Anything outside that, either dust with no real liquidity or a pool so large it is already an established, post-pump token, scores almost nothing. The shape of this scoring is a deliberate statement: the money is in pools big enough to be real but small enough to still move, and that "just right" region falls between Fibonacci landmarks.

The second signal is pool age, weighted thirty percent, and it is where the freshness obsession lives. Let T be the pool's age in seconds. A pool three seconds old or younger, three being the fourth Fibonacci number, gets a perfect age score, because three seconds is where Scematica's detector is faster than the overwhelming majority of competing bots. Five seconds or younger, the fifth Fibonacci number, is still in the golden window and scores nearly as high. Eight seconds, the sixth Fibonacci number, is acceptable. Thirteen seconds, the seventh Fibonacci number, is marginal, and it is the boundary beyond which first-mover advantage decays below break-even on average. Past thirteen seconds the age score collapses to almost nothing. The thirteen-second cliff is the single most important threshold in the protocol, and it is not arbitrary: it is the Fibonacci number that happens to mark the empirical edge of profitability.

The third signal is inflow velocity, weighted twenty-five percent, and this is where the golden ratio appears most directly. Velocity is simply the pool's size divided by its age, the average rate at which SOL has flowed in since creation. A velocity at or above twice the golden ratio, about 3.236 SOL per second, signals a crowd stampede and scores perfectly. A velocity at or above the golden ratio itself, about 1.618 SOL per second, signals confirmed momentum and scores strongly. The golden-ratio threshold is not chosen for elegance; it is the minimum rate at which a pool can sustain compound growth, because below it the organic buyers are not accumulating fast enough to overcome the sell pressure from the earliest participants cashing out. A velocity around one SOL per second is moderate, buyers are present but not decisively, and anything slower is weak.

The fourth signal is buy pressure, weighted ten percent, and it acts as an independent confirmation. Let R be the ratio of the quote reserve to the base reserve, the balance of the automated market maker. A ratio at or above the golden ratio means buyers have already pushed the SOL side of the pool to golden-ratio dominance, which confirms momentum from a completely different measurement than velocity. A ratio above one is above equilibrium. A ratio down near the inverse golden ratio, about 0.618, is only mildly skewed, and anything below that is balanced or sell-skewed and scores almost nothing. Buy pressure is the smallest weight because it is the noisiest signal, but when it agrees with velocity it turns two independent measurements into one strong conviction.


The composite score and the entry gate

The four signal scores are combined into a single number between zero and one by a simple weighted sum: thirty-five percent of the size score, thirty percent of the age score, twenty-five percent of the velocity score, and ten percent of the pressure score. That one number is the heart of the protocol. It is a compressed, calibrated verdict on the pool, and everything downstream, the decision to enter, the size of the position, and the signal handed to the learning agent, flows from it.

The default entry gate is a composite score of one half. A pool that scores at or above one half is a live candidate; a pool below it is rejected as a probable dead pool, with one deliberate exception, an exceptional velocity can bypass the gate on its own, because a genuine stampede is worth catching even when the other signals have not caught up. The choice of one half as the threshold is itself a balance: set it higher and the bot becomes too strict, passing only pools that are already parabolic, which as Scematica has learned the hard way leads directly to buying tops; set it lower and the dead pools flood back in. One half is the point where the protocol is discerning without being so picky that it only ever sees the peak.


Conviction-weighted position sizing

A good scoring system does not just decide whether to act; it decides how much to commit, and it commits more when it is more sure. The Fibonacci Protocol sizes positions by the composite score, and it does so, fittingly, with Fibonacci multipliers. A score in the top band, at or above 0.90, is an exceptional, near-perfect golden-ratio pattern and earns a doubled position. A score at or above 0.75 is a strong runner profile and earns a position scaled by the golden ratio itself, about 1.618 times the baseline. A score at or above one half is a moderate, acceptable entry and earns the baseline size. Weaker scores, if forced through at all, earn reduced size, three quarters or one half of baseline, so that the rare mistake costs less.

On top of the per-pool sizing sits a streak escalator that rewards a run of consecutive winning trades by scaling the next position by the raw Fibonacci number for the streak length, one, one, two, three, five, eight, thirteen, and so on, capped at twenty-one times to bound the leverage before it becomes reckless. Twenty-one is the ninth Fibonacci number, and the cap is a hard limit chosen so that even a hot streak cannot talk the bot into betting the account. This escalator is a direct expression of the asymmetry at the center of Scematica's edge: when the edge is clearly present, revealed by a run of wins, press it; when it is not, size down. The Fibonacci sequence provides a growth curve that presses aggressively but with a ceiling.


The exit ladder

Entering well is only half of a trade. The Fibonacci Protocol governs the exit with the same golden-ratio structure it uses for the entry, because the same crowd dynamics that build a pool also unwind it.

The exit ladder sets three take-profit targets as Fibonacci extensions of the entry price. The first target is a gain of about 61.8 percent, which is the golden ratio minus one, and at that level the protocol sells a portion of the position to lock in profit. The second target is a gain of about 161.8 percent, the golden ratio itself, where it sells a larger portion. The third target is about 261.8 percent, the golden ratio squared, where it sells the remainder. These are not round numbers chosen for comfort; they are the golden-ratio extensions that the winning trades tended to respect. Crucially, when velocity is still strong as a target is hit, the protocol does not simply sell; it escalates, promoting the next target to active so that a genuine runner is allowed to keep running rather than being cut short at an arbitrary level. This is the mechanism that protects the thin tail of enormous winners that produces most of Scematica's profit.

Two protective exits sit alongside the ladder. The first is the dead-pool exit: if a position does not gain at least two percent within five seconds of entry, it is classified as a dead pool and sold immediately, recycling the capital before it rots. Those numbers were themselves tuned against live data, loosened from an earlier, tighter three-second, five-percent rule that was found to be cutting slow-building winners and to be sitting above the automated-market-maker spread. The second is the golden retracement exit: if a position peaks and then gives back about 61.8 percent of its peak gain, the golden retracement, it is sold regardless of the absolute level, on the logic that a golden-ratio pullback from a peak is the signature of momentum genuinely reversing rather than merely pausing. Together these two exits enforce the discipline that the whole strategy depends on, take small losses quickly and lock in profits when the move is truly over, while the escalating ladder ensures the rare runner is never sold too early.


Fast-lane runner detection

Beyond the entry score, the protocol runs a second, lighter-weight signal during the life of a position, designed to catch the moment a merely-good pool reveals itself as a genuine runner. It fires a runner-detected signal when two conditions hold at once: the pool is still young, within the thirteen-second Fibonacci window, and its velocity is not just high but accelerating past 1.2 times the golden ratio, about 1.94, measured as the ratio of the pool's short-window inflow rate to its long-window rate. A velocity ratio around the golden ratio marks sustained Fibonacci momentum; a ratio above 1.2 times it marks acceleration, a pool that is not just moving but speeding up. This fast lane is what lets the escalating exit ladder know, in real time, that it is holding one of the rare tokens worth riding rather than one of the many worth exiting.


The one categorical override

The protocol is almost entirely quantitative, but it carries a single deliberate exception, and the exception is instructive because it encodes a piece of human judgment that no on-chain signal captures. When a project has purchased paid advertising on DexScreener, a token-tracking platform, the protocol treats that as strong evidence of legitimacy and bypasses both the Fibonacci entry gate and the separate statistical pool-score gate. The reasoning is behavioral rather than mathematical: a team that has spent verifiable real money on marketing has demonstrated commitment, has directed genuine traffic to the token, and, most tellingly, has done something rug-pull teams essentially never do, which is spend money on promotion before pulling the rug. A confirmed non-zero boost amount is therefore treated as a near-guaranteed buy, subject only to the hard on-chain fraud filters that can never be waived, such as a live freeze authority or an empty vault, and the standard exit rules. This override is a reminder that even a golden-ratio scoring system benefits from one channel through which real-world commitment can speak.


How the protocol enhances the AI

The Fibonacci Protocol was not built to replace Scematica's reinforcement-learning agent. It was built to make the agent smarter, and it does so in two distinct ways.

First, the protocol acts as a gate that shapes what the agent ever has to think about. The deep reinforcement-learning agent inside Scematica learns to size entries, to hold, and to veto buys, and like any learning system it learns faster and more reliably when the examples it sees are cleaner. By rejecting dead pools and obvious non-starters before the agent evaluates them, the Fibonacci gate raises the quality of the candidate stream, so the agent spends its learning capacity on the genuinely ambiguous cases, the pools that are plausibly good, rather than wasting it re-learning that dust never pumps. A pre-filter that removes the easy negatives is one of the most effective things you can do for a learning system, and that is precisely the role the entry gate plays.

Second, and more directly, the composite Fibonacci score is fed to the agent as one of its input features. The agent perceives each trading situation as a vector of two dozen normalized numbers, and the pool's Fibonacci score is one of them, sitting alongside the pool's age, its liquidity, the recent price change, the buy-to-sell ratio, and the velocity and acceleration signals. This means the agent does not have to rediscover the golden-ratio structure of good pools from raw data on its own; it is handed a compact, expert-calibrated summary of that structure and is free to learn how much to trust it, when to override it, and how to combine it with everything else it observes. The protocol, in effect, gives the agent a strong prior. The agent is not forced to obey the score, it can learn that in certain regimes a lower-scoring pool is worth a shot or a high-scoring one is a trap, but it starts from a signal that already contains most of the hard-won structure of the problem. A learning agent given a good feature converges faster and generalizes better than one asked to invent that feature from scratch, and the Fibonacci score is exactly that good feature.

Between the two roles, gate and feature, the protocol turns a firehose of anonymous new pools into a curated stream of scored candidates, which is the single most useful thing you can hand a system that is trying to learn to trade.


Calibration, and a note on honesty

It would be dishonest to present the Fibonacci Protocol as a law of nature. Two things are worth stating plainly. The parameter values, the weights, the score bands, the thresholds, are real and live in the code, but the empirical calibration figures, the win-rate tables and expectancy numbers that motivated each threshold, come from historical analysis of a limited window of trades, not from a constant of the universe. They describe what has worked, not what is guaranteed to keep working. And the golden-ratio theory, however elegant, is ultimately a lens for choosing where to place thresholds; its real justification is that thresholds placed at Fibonacci landmarks have out-performed thresholds placed at round numbers on the data Scematica has actually collected. The protocol earns its place by results, and it should continue to be re-calibrated as more live data arrives, exactly as its own dead-pool timing already was when three seconds proved too tight.


Conclusion

The Fibonacci Protocol is Scematica's answer to the hardest question in new-pool sniping: how to be both fast and discerning in the same three seconds, using nothing but the facts a brand-new pool reveals about itself. It answers by measuring the pool against the golden ratio and its Fibonacci landmarks in size, age, velocity, and buy pressure, compressing those four measurements into a single calibrated score, and using that score to gate entries, to size positions with Fibonacci confidence, to ladder exits along golden-ratio extensions, and, most importantly, to hand the reinforcement-learning agent a clean stream of candidates and a strong, expert-built feature to learn from. It is not magic, and it is not a guarantee. It is a structured, empirically-tuned way of recognizing the shape of a real pump early, which is the whole job, and it makes the machine that does the recognizing measurably better at its task than raw thresholds ever could.
