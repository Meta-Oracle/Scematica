# Scema-World — the campaign bible

The detail behind [SCEMA-WORLD-STORY.md](SCEMA-WORLD-STORY.md), which is the spine: premise, three
institutions, five acts, four roles. This document is the history, the people, the craft of the
forgery, the scene-level beats and the endings.

It is written under the same rule as the spine, which is the rule the whole project rests on:

> **The record may shape what you are told. It may never shape what you are paid.**

And a corollary the heavy hulls (`hulls.ts`) added, which is the same sentence pointed at the ship:

> **Your hull may shape what you are told. It may never shape what you are paid either.**

A dominion changes how a freehold speaks to you. It does not change a bounty by one salvage.

---

## Part I — How sealed observation became the basis of everything

The setting is not "the future with spaceships". It is a civilisation that had a specific
epistemic accident and built its institutions out of the wreckage. Every era below is one of the
software's actual guarantees, dramatised.

### The Long Disagreement

Before seals, every station kept its own map and every map was sincere. Nobody was lying. Charts
were compiled from whatever a hull had seen on its last run, merged by whoever was on the desk,
and copied outward. Two stations four days apart would hold two irreconcilable descriptions of the
same volume and neither could say which had drifted, because a chart carried no account of *how*
it had been made.

The period is remembered as tedious rather than tragic. Commerce worked. It simply cost twice what
it should have, because everything had to be checked on arrival.

### The Cascade

Then a relief convoy was routed through a volume on a chart that was confident and empty. Confident
because a survey had reported nothing there; empty because the survey had been cut short after
eleven hours and nobody had written that down. The chart could not distinguish *we looked and there
is nothing* from *we stopped looking*.

Four hundred and six hulls. The inquiry found no falsehood anywhere in the chain — every person who
had touched that chart had reported what they believed, and the belief was the problem.

The Cascade is the founding trauma and it is the correct one for this setting, because it was not
caused by a liar. **It was caused by a document with no way to say "I don't know".**

### The First Seal

The response was not a demand for truthfulness. Truthfulness is unenforceable and the Cascade had
demonstrated that it is also insufficient. The response was narrower and much stranger:

> A record shall be sealed at the moment of writing, and shall be unchangeable thereafter.

Not *true*. **Unchangeable.** You may still be wrong, and everyone will be able to see exactly what
you were wrong about, in the words you used at the time, with your name on it. The First Seal made
being wrong survivable and being *quietly* wrong impossible.

### The Rift Convention

The Seal was not enough, and the case that proved it is the **Meridian Silence**: a record, honest,
sealed, unedited, verifying perfectly to this day — which described a station, a market and four
lanes, and did not mention that the survey had been unable to read the volume behind the station,
because the observer had not thought the omission worth a line.

Nine hulls went into the volume behind the station.

The Rift Convention is the second and harder norm, and it is the one this civilisation actually
argues about:

> A record shall state what it could not read, in the same hand and under the same seal as what it
> could.

An observer who reports nothing in a place is making a claim. An observer who reports *that they
did not look* is making a different one. The Convention forced the second to be as legible as the
first — and the moment it did, the shape of a record changed. A well-made record is mostly an
account of its own limits, and a pilot reads those limits before they read anything else.

**This is why a rift is a lane that ends in nothing, one per blind spot, and why the count is
sacred.** It is the Convention rendered as a place you can fly to.

### The Charter of Narrow Claim

The Assay was chartered to do exactly one thing, and its charter opens with a sentence every
assessor can recite:

> *We prove nothing about the world. We prove that nothing moved.*

That sentence is the single most honest thing any institution in the setting has ever said, and it
is the source of every problem in the campaign, because nobody outside the Assay has read past the
first three words.

### The Compact

Carriage had to be neutral or the seals meant nothing — a record you had to trust the courier about
is a record you have to trust twice. The Couriers' Compact was founded on a refusal: they do not
read what they carry. It was a discipline before it was a virtue, and by the present day it has
become an identity, which is what makes Act IV lethal for them.

### Now

Three generations on, the charter is a plaque and the word on everybody's lips is **certified**.
Insurers price on it. Ports admit on it. Contracts specify it. And when a pilot says a record is
certified, what they mean, and what their counterparty hears, is *true*.

It never meant that. It has never once meant that. The instruments are not wrong and have never
been wrong; they are answering a question nobody is asking any more.

## Part II — The people

Nine names, each with a mechanical hook, because a character who only appears in text is a
character the game does not have.

### Assessor Ilaria Venn — Assay, three rings

Teaches Act I from a `CITADEL III`. She flies her own surveys, which is unusual at her tier, and
the first record the player ever loads is one of hers.

**It has four blind spots and she is not embarrassed about them.** That is the entire tutorial.
When the player asks what is behind the rifts, she says "I don't know, I ran out of fuel", and the
answer is delivered without apology, because in her profession that sentence is a *credential*.

Later she is the adjudicator in Act II who cannot choose between two verifying records of one
subject — and the reason the scene lands is that the player has already watched her be comfortable
with not knowing something. She is comfortable again here, and this time it is unbearable.

**Hook:** her record is the world the tutorial loads. Her blind spots are the first rifts the player
flies to.

### Deputy-Assessor Oren Kask — Assay, two rings

Certified more forged records than anybody else alive and has never forged one. He signs what
arrives, checks what the procedure says to check, and the procedure says to check that the record
verifies. It always does.

He is the campaign's most important character and he never does anything. When the player finally
confronts him he is not defensive, he is *confused*: he did his job precisely. He is right. That is
the horror.

**Hook:** the seat that certified the Act III sector. Visiting it is how the player learns the
forgery was not smuggled past anybody.

### Sabine Orr — Couriers' Compact

Carries records between seats and does not read them, until the day a hold breach forces her to
open one to check whether it survived, and she reads two lines.

She then has to deliver it, because that is what carriage means, and she does.

**Hook:** the Act II delivery contract is hers to hand over. In Act IV she is the first blue
silhouette the player sees fired on.

### Teodor Vail — "the Cartwright"

Not the workshop's owner. Its **teacher**. Vail is the person who worked out that a forgery's
problem is not making a world up — anyone can do that — but making up an *ignorance* that looks
like it was suffered rather than designed.

He is proud of it in the specific way of a craftsman whose craft has no honest use. When the player
meets him he explains the method, in detail, without being asked, because nobody has ever been able
to appreciate it before.

**Hook:** the Act IV sector is his masterwork, and it is the one the player cannot break by reading.

### Halden Reeve — Freehold captain, formerly Assay

Their survey reported a derelict where a client's prospectus said market. The record was refused
certification on a procedural ground that was technically correct. They took it to the Freeholds and
have been there since.

Reeve is the campaign's accidental villain: to argue that certification was worthless they published
a proof that a record could be *entirely true, honestly sealed, properly rift-marked* and still a
lie, by choosing where to stand before you look. They published it as a warning.

The workshop read it as a manual.

**Hook:** commands a freehold capital. In a light hull they are unapproachable; in a capital of your
own they will talk, which is the only place the ship you fly changes anything at all — and it
changes what you are *told*.

### Marshal-Captain Iseult Brand — the patrol

Runs the yellow wing in whatever sector you are in. She does not care about the plot. She hunts
raiders whether or not anyone is watching, and she is already doing it before the player arrives.

She exists so the setting has one person in it who is simply doing a job well, and so the sector
keeps moving during the acts. **She must never be given a quest.**

**Hook:** none. That is the hook. `respawn.ts` and `factions.ts` already run her war; the campaign
names it and touches nothing.

### The observer

Every world the player loads has an `observer` field, and it is a real string written by a real
producer. The campaign reads it and addresses them.

Where the record came from `RepoObserver`, they are a surveyor. Where it came from the browser
extension, they are somebody who looked at a page. Where it came from `alchem_link.omni`, they read
oracles. The game does not explain the joke and does not need to: what it says is *somebody looked
at something and wrote down what they saw, and you are inside the writing.*

**Hook:** the logbook's first line, in every world, is the observer's own identifier.

### Two more, unmet

**The client.** Every forged record was commissioned. The campaign never identifies who paid, and
the refusal is deliberate — see *What must never happen*.

**The first forger.** Predates Vail, unknown, possibly a legend. Their supposed record is the
Freeholds' relic and may itself be a forgery, which is the joke the Freeholds tell about themselves.

## Part III — The craft of forgery, in three generations

This is the campaign's engine. Each generation defeats the previous generation's detection method,
each is a real property of the record format, and the third one cannot be beaten — which is what
makes the ending honest.

### First generation — the world that knows everything

A forger writes a place they never went. Since they are inventing it, **there is nothing in it they
failed to see.** Every signal `measured: true`. No blind spots. `extent.total` a confident number,
because they drew the boundary.

It seals correctly. It verifies forever. And it is the most comfortable sector anybody has ever
flown, because it has no rifts, no ghosts, and sensor range to the edges.

**The tell:** a record that claims to know everything. In the game this is *immediate and visual* —
the sky is wrong by being tidy.

**Defeated in Act III**, and it is the amateur's mistake. Vail's whole contribution is noticing it.

### Second generation — manufactured ignorance

Vail's method. Put the rifts back in. Mark signals `measured: false`. Cap the extent.

It defeats the first-generation tell completely, and everybody who learned Act III's lesson now
reads these records as *scrupulous*. There is a period in the setting where the most trusted records
in circulation are the forged ones, because they are the most carefully hedged.

**The tell, and it is subtle enough to carry an act:** real ignorance is shaped by *mechanism*.
An observer's blind spots cluster around how they were looking — a permissions boundary, a walk that
hit a cap, a feed that timed out, a volume behind a station they had no fuel to reach. The gaps come
in a *kind*, and the kind is usually boring and usually inconvenient to the observer themselves.

Invented ignorance is shaped by *topic*. It is placed where a question would be awkward. Vail's
sectors are unreadable in precisely the volumes where a buyer would want to look, and legible
everywhere else, and each individual gap is perfectly plausible.

So the detective question moves, and it is a much better question:

> **Is the shape of what they did not see explicable by how they were looking?**

In the game: fly the rifts. In an honest sector they are *arbitrary* — four gaps in a line because
the observer ran out of fuel on one heading, or a cluster on one flank because that is where the
sensor was blind. In a Vail sector each rift sits neatly over something a client would rather not
have surveyed, and the gaps have no common cause. **Ignorance with a motive has no mechanism.**

**Defeated in Act IV**, and it takes a person rather than an instrument. Nothing computes this. The
player works it out by flying, and Vail confirms it by explaining, because he wants somebody to
understand.

### Third generation — the honest forgery

Reeve's proof, which the workshop turned into a product.

Do not invent anything. Go to the volume. Observe it properly. Report your blind spots truthfully.
Seal it correctly.

**Choose where to stand.**

A survey run from the far side of a station legitimately cannot read what is behind it. A walk begun
at the wrong node legitimately terminates before it reaches the derelicts. A pass timed for one hour
rather than another legitimately sees a market busy or empty. Every claim is true. Every blind spot
is genuine, mechanism-shaped, and would survive Vail's tell. The record is *correct*.

And it is a lie, because the observer knew what standing there would fail to show.

**There is no tell.** None. Not one instrument in this setting, and not one line of code in this
repository, can distinguish a third-generation forgery from an honest survey by an unlucky observer
— because there is nothing to distinguish. They are the same artefact. The difference is entirely in
an intention that was never written down.

This is the campaign's floor and it is a true statement about the software:

> **Verification bounds tampering. It has never bounded intent, and no amount of it ever will.**

**Not defeated. Ever.** Act V is about what you do with a problem that has no solution, and the
answer is the only one that has ever worked for this class of problem: **more than one observer, of
the same subject, who did not coordinate.** Two independently-stood surveys of one volume disagree
in ways a single one cannot hide, and the chain of custody has to be inspectable by someone the
authors do not control.

Which is `scema anchor`, and it is why the record format was building toward it the whole time.

## Part IV — The acts, scene by scene

Each beat lists its **trigger** — a thing the game already detects — because a beat that needs new
simulation is a beat that will not ship.

### Act I — *The frozen sky*

**Setting:** whatever world the player loaded, framed as Venn's survey.

| # | Beat | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The logbook opens with the record's own `observer` string, and Venn's note: *this is what I saw, and here is what I did not.* | world loaded |
| 2 | First rift approach. *A lane that ends is not a wall. It is where nobody looked.* | within sensor range of a rift node |
| 3 | First ghost. *This contact was estimated. It may not be here. That is not a fault in your sensors.* | first `measured: false` contact resolved |
| 4 | First phantom refusal. The player routes to a station and it will not refuel them, because it was modelled and never seen. | a service refusal at a `phantom` node |
| 5 | Venn's four gaps, named. She is asked and says *I ran out of fuel*, with no apology at all. | all four rifts visited, or the logbook opened after beat 2 |

**What Act I teaches:** the sky is somebody's testimony, and its holes are the honest part.

### Act II — *Two true maps of one place*

**Setting:** a carriage contract, handed over by Sabine Orr.

| # | Beat | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The contract. Carry a sealed record between seats. You may verify it; you may not read it. | quest accepted (`quests.ts` haul, subject a citadel) |
| 2 | En route, a second courier is carrying a record of the **same subject**, sealed a week earlier. | a scripted courier contact on the haul route |
| 3 | Both verify. The player can run the verifier on both, for real, in `/omni`. | player exports either record |
| 4 | Venn, adjudicating, declines to choose. *My instrument says neither has been edited. It has nothing else to say.* | delivery at a `CITADEL III` |
| 5 | Logbook: *certified never meant true. It has never once meant true.* | act closes |

**What Act II teaches:** the gap between what the Assay proves and what everybody hears.

### Act III — *The comfortable sector*

**Setting:** a first-generation forgery, shipped as a fixture so it is the same suspicious sector for
everybody.

| # | Beat | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arrival. Sensor range to the edges. Every contact solid. **No rifts.** | world loaded, `blind_spots === 0` |
| 2 | The logbook notices before the player does: *this record claims to have seen everything.* | same |
| 3 | The ghosts are real. In an honest record a ghost is an estimate; here they were written, so they are solid, and they are hostile. | first contact engagement in the fixture |
| 4 | Kask's seat certified it, correctly, by a procedure he followed exactly. | dock at the certifying citadel |
| 5 | Leaving costs more than the sector paid. **It must.** | act closes |

**What Act III teaches:** the em-dash rule inverted. The things the record described are supposed to
be the uncertain ones; here the described things are the only real ones.

### Act IV — *Provenance is a chain of who said so*

**Setting:** a second-generation forgery — Vail's masterwork. It has rifts. It is scrupulous. It is
the most trustworthy-looking sector in the game.

| # | Beat | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arrival, and the logbook is *reassured*. Blind spots present and properly marked. | world loaded, `blind_spots > 0` |
| 2 | The gaps have no common cause. Fly three and the player is asked, for the first time, to notice a *shape* rather than a value. | three rifts visited in the fixture |
| 3 | Each gap sits over something. The player works it out by flying, not by reading a number. | fourth rift visited |
| 4 | Vail explains, unprompted and at length, because nobody has ever appreciated the work. | dock at the workshop node |
| 5 | The Compact breaks. Couriers start being fired on by both sides, and the sector gets quieter. | first courier destroyed by a non-player faction |
| 6 | Sabine Orr, specifically. | scripted |

**What Act IV teaches:** ignorance with a motive has no mechanism. And: neutral carriage of a
forgery is how a forgery becomes consensus.

### Act V — *What you do about it*

**Setting:** a third-generation record. True, honest, correctly rift-marked, and a lie.

| # | Beat | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reeve, who published the proof as a warning and watched it become a product. | dock at a freehold citadel |
| 2 | The player applies every tell they have learned and every one of them passes. | any verification attempt in the fixture |
| 3 | Stated plainly, once: there is no instrument for this and there never will be. | act opens |
| 4 | The only remedy: independent observations of one subject, and a chain of custody nobody in it controls. | quest — deliver an uncertified second survey of the Act V subject to a three-ring seat |
| 5 | Anchoring. The roots go somewhere the authors do not own. | act closes |

**What Act V teaches:** you cannot fix this, and there is still something worth doing.

## Part V — Four roles, one set of events

The role decides who shoots at you (`roles.ts`) and it should decide the story just as completely.
Same acts, same events, four irreconcilable readings.

| | **Bounty hunter** | **Trader** | **Smuggler** | **Pirate** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | An Assay contract. The instructor is your employer. | A carriage contract. The instructor is a client. | You are flying somebody else's licence. | You are inside a seal you did not pay for. |
| **II** | You escort the courier. | You *are* the courier. | You carry the second record — the uncertified one. | You know what both are worth and neither is worth stealing. |
| **III** | You are sent, and told it is safe. | You are routed through, and it is profitable. | You are told to avoid it, which is how you know. | You raid it, and nothing here is worth anything, which is the tell. |
| **IV** | Ordered to bring in Reeve, whose only crime was refusing to seal something. | You discover some of them were in your hold. | Both sides hunt you and one of them is right. | You can break the workshop yourself. |
| **V** | Take the contract, or lose every Assay board in the game. | Keep carrying, or start reading. | Force an uncertified record into the chain of custody. | Destroy the seals, and the evidence with them. |

### The endings, named

- **Bounty hunter — *The Warrant*.** Take it and the Assay keeps working, with you as the reason it
  can. Refuse and every citadel board closes; only the freeholds will deal with you. The game does
  not warn you which one refusal is, and it does not tell you afterwards which was right.
- **Trader — *The Manifest*.** Keep carrying and stay useful to everyone including the workshop.
  Start reading and you are a smuggler, permanently — the role changes and the boards change with it.
- **Smuggler — *The Testimony*.** The strongest ending available. An uncertified record forced into
  the chain of custody, where it can be **argued with**, which is the most any record can hope for.
  It does not become true. It becomes contested, which is better.
- **Pirate — *The Bonfire*.** Break the workshop and destroy the only evidence the forgeries were
  forgeries. The sector is freer and less knowable. The game does not tell you this is bad.

Not one of them solves the problem. The problem does not have a solution — that is Part III — and an
ending that supplied one would be the campaign lying about the tool the whole story is about.

## Part VI — How the hulls read

The heavy tiers arrived as a systems change and they are worth a paragraph here, because a capital
is a *political object* in a setting where records are testimony.

- A **light hull** is a pilot. Nobody has an opinion about you.
- A **medium** is a business. Freeholds price you differently; the patrol notices you.
- A **capital** is a claim. The Assay does not certify capital pilots — its charter has nothing to
  say about tonnage — so a private dreadnought is, by construction, *outside the thing that grants
  legitimacy here*. The Freeholds find this delightful. Reeve will only talk to you in one.

**And none of it moves a number.** No bounty, no salvage, no contract reward, no price reads
`ship.frame`. The rule that stops a record being worth forging is the same rule that stops a hull
being worth grinding for the wrong reason: it may change what you are told and never what you are
paid. `check:scemaworld` already scans the reward path for record fields; the campaign's modules
must be added to that scan, and `ship.frame` must be added to what it scans *for*.

## Part VII — What must never happen to this

The spine lists six. These are the ones the deeper material adds, and each is a specific way to
break something already paid for.

7. **Never let the player detect a third-generation forgery.** Not with a rare item, not with a
   perceptive NPC, not with a skill check. The whole point is that the artefact is indistinguishable
   from an honest one *because it is one*. A detection mechanic here would be the fiction claiming a
   capability the software does not have, in the one place the software's honesty is the subject.
8. **Never identify the client.** Every forgery was commissioned, and naming the buyer converts a
   structural problem — certification is a business, and a certifier who refuses too much loses the
   seat — into a personal one with a villain who can be shot. The setting's failure is that a
   procedure followed correctly produced a catastrophe. A villain would let the player off.
9. **Never make Kask culpable.** He followed the procedure exactly. If he is corrupt, the campaign
   is about corruption, which is a much smaller and much more comfortable story than the one where
   everybody did their job.
10. **Never give the marshals a quest.** Brand's war runs whether or not anyone is watching, and that
    is the sector's texture (`respawn.ts`). The moment the patrol is waiting for the player, the
    world stops being a place and becomes a set.
11. **Never let Act III or Act IV pay well.** If a forged sector is ever the one players farm, the
    campaign has recreated the exact incentive the entire design exists to remove. Both fixtures must
    cost more than they yield, and `check:scemaworld` should assert it the way it asserts everything
    else about the reward path.
12. **Never resolve the two gaps `verify` leaves open.** No beat may let the player prove a record is
    *true*, or prove it is the *original*. Anchoring is the honest partial answer and it is Act V's
    whole subject.

## Part VIII — Implementation, smallest first

Nothing here is built. The order matters: each item works alone and the first two are most of the
value.

1. **`lib/scemaworld/story.ts`** — pure. Acts, beats, and one predicate per beat. Reads the record
   as freely as it likes; returns text and flags, and **exports nothing any payout path imports**.
   Add it to the reward-path source scan with an *inverted* assertion: it may read record fields, and
   nothing in `ship.ts` / `claim.ts` / `quests.ts` may read it.
2. **Three fixtures**, and they are the campaign's keystone:
   - `forgery-naive.json` — zero blind spots, every signal measured, confident extent.
   - `forgery-vail.json` — blind spots present, mechanism-*less*: each gap over a subject, no common
     cause. The hard one to author, and the one the second act is made of.
   - `survey-honest.json` — a true, correctly-marked record whose observer stood in the wrong place.
     Byte-for-byte indistinguishable in kind from an honest survey, because it is one.

   All three must **verify**, and `check:scemaworld` should assert that they verify *while being
   false* — which is the single most important test in the campaign, since it is the claim the entire
   story makes about the tool.
3. **Beat state on `GameState`**, beside `quests`. Persisted per record, unlike the account
   (`wallet.ts`), because the story is about *that* world while the wallet is about you.
4. **A logbook panel**, sharing the market's tab bar. Beats accumulate; readable any time; no modal
   and no pause — the sector keeps simulating, which is rule 6 of the spine.
5. **Dialogue at citadels, gated by tier.** A one-ring outpost cannot advance a beat that needs a
   seal. The rings already say who can do what (`generate.ts::CITADELS`); the story should mean it.

`story.ts` and the three fixtures are independently testable without a GPU, in the same way
`quests.ts` is. Everything else is presentation.
